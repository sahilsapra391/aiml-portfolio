Three Ways Data
Breaks a Model

Missing values, a half-percent positive class, and a live model decaying in production — three escalating scenarios diagnosed under an AI coach that pushed back on my calls.

Field 01 — Introduction

What this artifact is

This is a set of three applied data challenges worked through with SchoolAI's Data Challenge Scenario Coach. Each one hands you a broken situation instead of a clean dataset: a table full of holes, a class ratio of 200 to 1, and a deployed model losing accuracy in production. There is no code to submit and no single correct answer. You make the call, explain the reasoning, name the trade-off, and defend it when the coach disagrees. What follows is the case log.

Field 02 — Description

The case log

Three scenarios, escalating in difficulty. Each brief is summarized from the coach's prompt; each call and its reasoning are my answers from the live session, tightened for reading. The screenshots in the Evidence section show the originals.

Case 01 Beginner 15% + 8% missing

House prices with holes in the data

A price model built on square footage, bedrooms, year built, and neighborhood. After collection, 15% of rows are missing year built and another 8% are missing bedroom count. The missingness appears random, with no obvious tie to price.

The call

Impute rather than drop, and treat the two columns differently: neighborhood-level median for year built, mode for bedrooms. Add a binary was_missing flag on both columns. Then validate the whole thing against a dropped-rows baseline.

Why

If the two gaps barely overlap, dropping rows costs close to a quarter of the dataset, which is far too expensive for the accuracy it buys back. Year built and neighborhood move together, since houses on the same street usually went up around the same time, so a neighborhood median preserves signal that a global median would flatten. The flags cost nothing, and if missingness turns out to carry information the model gets to use it. The baseline comparison is the step people skip, and it is the only thing that proves imputation actually helped instead of just filling space.

Where the coach pushed back

I had offered median or mode for bedrooms as interchangeable. The coach argued mode is the right default for a discrete count, since a median can land on something like 2.5 bedrooms that does not exist in the real world. Fair, and I took it further on myself: I had accepted "the missingness appears random" at face value. That is a hypothesis to test, not a given. If year built goes missing more often on older properties, every choice above changes.

Case 02 Intermediate 5,000 of 1,000,000

Fraud detection where the signal is half a percent

One million card transactions, 5,000 of them fraudulent. The business wants fraud caught, but it is equally sensitive to false positives: flagging a legitimate transaction frustrates the customer and costs real money to investigate.

The call

Throw out accuracy immediately. Evaluate on precision-recall AUC rather than ROC. Reach for class weights before SMOTE, and if I do resample, only inside the training fold. Gradient boosting with scale_pos_weight. Then tune the decision threshold against expected dollar cost.

Why

A model that predicts "never fraud" is 99.5% accurate and completely worthless, so accuracy is the first thing to go. ROC flatters at this ratio, because 995,000 negatives make any false positive rate look tiny; a precision-recall curve puts the pressure exactly where the business feels it. SMOTE applied before the split leaks synthetic neighbors of test rows into training and produces a validation score that dies in production. Class weights are simpler and do not invent data. Gradient boosting handles the imbalance natively and still gives me feature importance, which matters when someone asks why their card got declined.

The honest answer, though, is that sampling is not the real lever. The threshold is. Put a dollar figure on a missed fraud versus an investigation, then tune to minimize expected cost instead of defaulting to 0.5 because that is what the library does.

Case 03 Advanced AUC 0.89 → 0.76

A churn model quietly decaying in production

Trained on six months of history, validated at 0.89 AUC, deployed. Three months in, AUC is 0.76 and precision on the high-risk segment has fallen off. Meanwhile the company launched in two new markets with different demographics, a pricing change landed six weeks ago, and feature distributions have shifted.

The call

Separate covariate shift from concept drift before touching the model. Slice performance by market and by pre/post-pricing cohort. Do not train one model across two pricing regimes. Monitor input distributions, not just AUC. Retrain on trigger rather than on the calendar, and keep a rollback path.

Why

The two new markets are covariate shift: the inputs moved, but the relationship between behavior and churn probably did not. The pricing change is concept drift, where the relationship itself changed. Those need different fixes, and a single blended retrain hides both. Slicing is what tells me which one I am actually looking at, and whether it is both at once.

Watching only AUC means finding out months late, after the damage. Watching input distributions catches the shift as it happens. Calendar retraining either burns compute on nothing or misses the event that mattered. On balancing stability against responsiveness, I would make monitoring fast and retraining slow: alert on distribution shift right away, but require a triggered threshold plus a shadow-mode evaluation before a new model takes live traffic, with a rollback ready, because the fresh model can always be worse than the one it replaced.

Field 03 — Objective

Why I did it

The first three artifacts in this portfolio show that I can build, choose, and explain. This one exists to show that I can diagnose. My objective was to work problems where the data is the obstacle rather than the input, and to practice the part of the job that actually decides whether a model survives contact with production: naming what is wrong, choosing a fix that holds up against a business constraint, and being explicit about the trade-off I am accepting.

Field 04 — Process

How the session went

  1. Entered the Space. Joined the course's SchoolAI Space for the Data Challenge Scenario Coach, which runs three scenarios in escalating difficulty.
  2. Worked each case cold. No lookups. Read the scenario, made the call, wrote out the reasoning, and named the trade-off before the coach responded.
  3. Took the pushback. The coach challenged my imputation choice on Case 01. I conceded the point, then flagged a larger assumption of my own that it had not caught.
  4. Escalated. The cases moved from a single-table cleanup problem, to an evaluation-metric problem, to a production monitoring problem. Each one added a constraint the previous case did not have.
  5. Captured and distilled. Screenshotted the session end to end, then reworked the three answers into the case log on this page, keeping the reasoning intact and cutting the filler.

Field 06 — Value Proposition

Why this artifact matters

Getting a model to 0.89 in a notebook is the easy part. The job starts when a quarter of the rows are incomplete, the positive class is half a percent, and the number quietly slides to 0.76 three months after launch.

PM Takeaway: Tying threshold tuning to the actual dollar cost of a false positive ensures the model optimizes for operational KPIs, not just abstract accuracy.
Field 07 — Unique Value

What makes it different

Most coursework evidence shows the right answer arriving on the first try. This one keeps a call I got partly wrong. The mode-versus-median correction is small on its own, but the bigger miss was mine to catch: I had taken "the missingness appears random" at face value instead of treating it as something to verify. Finding your own weak assumption before someone else does is the habit I would want a hiring manager to see, which is exactly why the pushback stayed in rather than getting edited out.

Field 08 — Relevance

How it connects to my value proposition

My value proposition is turning ambiguous AI opportunities into working, tested products, and ambiguity in AI work almost always shows up as a data problem first. All three of these cases are things I have watched play out on real teams: the field nobody validated, the metric that flattered a useless model, the dashboard that looked fine because nobody was watching the inputs. It also links back to the valuation models discussed in Artifact 2: even a perfectly selected linear regression will decay if the underlying demographic inputs shift without triggering a retraining alert.
